The Bible Story — Retold

An original simplified retelling, not an official Bible translation. Read Matthew 2:1–12 for the Gospel wording.

After Jesus was born in Bethlehem, travelers called Magi came from the East to Jerusalem. They had seen a star and believed it meant that a new king had been born. They wanted to find him and honor him.

King Herod was troubled by their questions. He asked religious scholars where the promised ruler was expected to be born. They pointed to Bethlehem. Herod secretly asked the Magi to tell him when the star had appeared and told them to report back when they found the child.

The Magi followed the star and were filled with joy when they reached the place where Jesus was. Matthew says they entered a house, saw the child with Mary his mother, bowed down and honored him. They offered three kinds of gifts: gold, frankincense and myrrh.

God warned the Magi in a dream not to return to Herod, so they traveled home by a different route. Herod's stated wish to worship Jesus did not match his intentions, as the following part of Matthew's story shows.

Traditionally, artists paint three Magi as kings, often wearing crowns and magnificent robes. Matthew does not actually state how many Magi there were or name them as kings. The familiar number three probably reflects the three gifts. The wise men's names and royal crowns belong to later Christian traditions.

Some artists combine the Magi's visit with the shepherds' visit in a single Nativity painting. Others add saints or people who commissioned the artwork. This can make a picture meaningful even when it is not a literal reconstruction of the Gospel.

Different artists may combine events or add symbols, prophets and later saints. We can appreciate those choices while noticing what the Gospel actually says.

Three Kings: What Does Matthew Actually Say?

Matthew 2:1–12 mentions visitors from the East, a star, Herod, and three kinds of gifts: gold, frankincense and myrrh. It does not tell us their exact number, give their personal names, or call them kings. The familiar picture of three crowned kings grew through later Christian tradition.

When studying a painting, try making two columns: Gospel detail and later tradition. Gold and the star belong in the first column. Exactly three visitors all wearing crowns belong in the second. Neither artistic tradition nor religious symbolism needs to be mistaken for a literal line of the Bible.

Another art-history term to learn is tondo, Italian for a round painting. Fra Angelico and Fra Filippo Lippi's Adoration is a tondo. The word describes its shape, not its biblical subject.

Domenico Ghirlandaio — Adoration of the Magi (Ospedale degli Innocenti)

1488–14892 identified saints/prophets

Two distinct saints named John join the Magi. The altarpiece was created for a Florentine institution devoted to caring for children.

A Little Art History

Ghirlandaio painted this large altarpiece for Florence's Hospital of the Innocents, an institution associated with the care of children.

The figures include visitors from the East, but also historical patrons and saints connected with the hospital. A painting could honor its commissioners while teaching a Bible story.

John the Baptist and John the Evangelist are two different people. Their presence is a devotional addition, not a claim that both appeared in Matthew's Magi account.

Fra Angelico and Fra Filippo Lippi — Adoration of the Magi

c. 1440–1460Story and symbols

A long procession leads to Mary and Jesus in a circular panel called a tondo. Look for kneeling visitors and gifts.

A Little Art History

This painting is a tondo, the Italian name for a circular work of art. Both Fra Angelico and Fra Filippo Lippi contributed to it.

Crowds of visitors and animals wind through the landscape toward the Holy Family, leading our eyes to the three Magi in the foreground.

Matthew describes the Magi and their gifts, but the elaborate procession and architectural ruins are artistic additions that help make the scene feel significant.

Peter Paul Rubens — Adoration of the Magi

1624–1625Story and symbols

Vivid color and movement make the visitors and their gifts prominent. Compare this with earlier Renaissance depictions.

A Little Art History

Rubens was a leading Baroque artist. His richly colored figures seem to move and react, creating a very different feeling from earlier, more orderly paintings.

The Magi's clothing and gifts draw attention to the visit of travelers who came from the East to honor Jesus.

Matthew mentions gold, frankincense and myrrh, but does not identify exactly three visitors or call them kings. Artists developed those traditions over centuries.
