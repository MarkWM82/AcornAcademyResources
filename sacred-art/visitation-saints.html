How to Identify the Main Figures

Mary is the younger relative arriving after Gabriel's announcement. Elizabeth is the older relative expecting John the Baptist. They frequently embrace or clasp hands, but artists can show the moment just before the embrace too.

Look for the relationship and the story, not only a blue robe, a veil, a gray head of hair, a position on the left, or a particular pose. Try identifying the two figures when you see a new painting, without first reading its title.

Mary

May be visibly pregnant, younger, traveling, or receiving a welcome. Her role in Luke's story and her connection with the unborn Jesus identify her more reliably than color alone.

Elizabeth

May be older, pregnant, welcoming the traveler, or standing outside Zechariah's home. None of those clues in isolation is unique; together in a Visitation scene they are strong evidence.

The Unborn Children

Mary carries Jesus and Elizabeth carries John the Baptist. Luke describes John leaping when Elizabeth hears Mary's voice. A later baptism of Jesus by the grown John is a separate episode decades afterward—Raphael paints it in the distance. We should not describe the two unborn babies as people visibly standing among Mary and Elizabeth.

Recognizing Zechariah

Zechariah is Elizabeth's husband and John's father. Luke describes him as a priest. In Rogier van der Weyden's Visitation, a small older man appears near Elizabeth's home in the background; in Rembrandt's painting, an older man is helped down the steps.

Neither a beard nor old age is an exclusive attribute of Zechariah. To recognize him, use the location, his family relationship to Elizabeth and John, and reliable collection descriptions. When he isn't shown, don't invent his presence.

Ghirlandaio's Two Additional Marys

Domenico Ghirlandaio's Louvre painting includes two saints who are not the women greeting one another: Mary Jacobi (left) and Mary Salome (right). The Louvre records identifying inscriptions at the top of the picture. That is why the museum can name them.

These two figures do not have an obvious, unique object here comparable to Saint Catherine's wheel or Saint Margaret's dragon. Recognizing them across unfamiliar paintings requires context, inscriptions, and the other people present. We can teach their names, but should not pretend that clothing alone is sufficient.

Mary Jacobi

A named holy woman from Christian tradition, traditionally connected with women who follow Jesus and witness events surrounding his death and burial.

Mary Salome

Another holy woman associated with Gospel traditions about the women who followed Jesus. She is distinct from Mary, the mother of Jesus.

Are All the Other Women Saints?

No. In Pontormo's Carmignano painting, two women stand behind Mary and Elizabeth. Art historians have interpreted them as attendants or as a visual echo of the central figures. Their specific names are not established. Giotto also shows women near the main encounter who need not be identified as named saints.

Being a person in a sacred painting does not automatically make a figure a saint. Knowing when an identification is uncertain is part of learning to identify people responsibly.

Practice Across Paintings

In Albertinelli, the figures embrace beneath an arch. Which clues matter?

They are a younger visitor and an older relative in the Visitation, shown in the moment of greeting. Position or cloak color isn't sufficient by itself.

Bloch shows a younger woman approaching steps and an older woman raising her arms. Is this still a Visitation?

Yes. The embrace has not happened yet, but the action and family relationship still identify Mary and Elizabeth.

In Ghirlandaio, how do we know the two side saints' names?

The upper inscriptions and collection history, not any uniquely identifying robe colors or gestures.

What if four women appear in Pontormo's painting?

Mary and Elizabeth are the embracing foreground pair. The figures behind them are not securely named.

How can you recognize Zechariah in a new depiction?

Use the biblical setting and relationship to Elizabeth and John, together with a trustworthy inscription or collection identification. Old age is not enough.

Try Recognizing Them in a Different Story

Once the children recognize Mary, Elizabeth, Zechariah and John in Visitation paintings, a useful next challenge is Federico Barocci's Madonna of the Cat (late sixteenth century). It depicts Elizabeth visiting Mary after Jesus and John have been born, alongside Joseph and Zechariah. That changes the children's ages, the setting and the action while retaining their family relationships.

This painting isn't part of the current local image collection or quiz bank. It would be an excellent later test of genuine recognition rather than memorization of a particular Visitation.
