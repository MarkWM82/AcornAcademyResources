Why Are Saints in Nativity Paintings?

Some people pictured in religious paintings lived centuries after Jesus was born. Artists include them to honor the saints, the community, or the people who commissioned the paintings. This does not mean those saints were actually standing beside the manger according to Luke.

Saints often have recognizable attributes: objects such as a bell, a wheel, a jar or a spear. The attributes help viewers know who is being shown.

Try to identify the saint from the object before reading the person's name. In the game, questions use close-up images to make small details easier to see.

The Meaning Behind Each Attribute

Recognizing Catherine’s wheel or Mary Magdalene’s ointment jar is the first step. The wheel recalls Catherine’s martyrdom legend; the jar relates to traditions of anointing; Mary’s blue and red clothing often carries theological meaning. Understanding why the attribute appears helps learners recognize a saint in other paintings.

Before the Quiz: How to Recognize Saints

Identifying a saint is a bit like solving a mystery. You look for repeated attributes, check the scene and the other figures, and—when there is writing—read it. Not every saint has a single unmistakable appearance, and not every artist uses the same attributes.

1. Look for an attribute

A wheel, a bell, a dragon, an ointment jar, a spear, or a book can be clues. A single object is not always enough by itself.

2. Ask who is nearby

Saint Anne often appears with the young Mary; patron saints can stand behind members of a family who share their names.

3. Compare several pictures

For example, Anthony the Abbot and Anthony of Padua are different people with different traditional attributes.

4. Check the source

An inscription quoting a saint's words or a biblical book can be much stronger evidence than clothing, hairstyle or age alone.

Recognizing Saint Anne Across Paintings

According to Christian tradition, Anne is Mary's mother and Jesus' grandmother. Artists often show her as a mature woman caring for Mary. A very familiar subject is Saint Anne teaching the young Mary to read: look for an older woman, a girl, and an open book. Some artists show Anne with Mary and Jesus together, representing three generations.

Important limitation: an older woman in a Nativity is not automatically Anne. Age, a veil, and clothing are not unique identifying symbols. In Matthias Stom's Raleigh painting the museum identifies the older woman as Anne, but we should combine the painting's history with the saint's family relationship. That is different from guessing Anne in every picture of an elderly woman.

Why Catherine and Peter Martyr Are in Fra Angelico's Nativity

Fra Angelico's fresco was made for a Dominican convent. Alongside Jesus, Mary and Joseph are two saints who lived many centuries afterward. They are there to help the friars reflect on holy lives, not because Luke says they attended the birth.

Saint Catherine of Alexandria: tradition describes her as a learned woman of royal status. In the San Marco fresco you can recognize her crown. In other pictures she often has a spiked wheel, recalling her martyrdom legend; sometimes she carries a sword or a book. The wheel is not clearly visible in this particular fresco.

Saint Peter Martyr (Peter of Verona): a thirteenth-century Dominican preacher who was killed because of his religious work. Artists may show the Dominican black-and-white habit, a wound or blade at his head, or a palm of martyrdom. He is not Peter the Apostle, who is frequently shown with keys. It makes sense to see this saint in a Dominican convent, even if he is less familiar than the apostles.

How Donors and Their Name Saints Help Us

A donor is someone who paid for or commissioned a painting. Donors sometimes asked artists to include their patron saints—saints whose names they shared or whose protection they sought. In the Portinari Triptych the family kneels much smaller than the saints behind them.

On the left, the father Tommaso appears with Saint Thomas the Apostle, whose spear recalls martyrdom. The boy Antonio is connected to Saint Anthony the Abbot, recognizable by a bell, staff, and often a pig in other pictures.

On the right, Maria and their daughter Margherita appear with Saint Margaret of Antioch, linked with a dragon, and Mary Magdalene, traditionally recognized by a jar of ointment. The names and objects together make identification much stronger than clothing alone.

Two Different Saints Named Anthony

Saint Anthony the Abbot

An Egyptian desert monk of the third and fourth centuries, often pictured as an older bearded hermit. Look for a T-shaped staff (tau cross), a bell, sometimes a pig, or scenes of the temptations he faced.

See attributes in the National Gallery guide

Saint Anthony of Padua

A Franciscan friar from the thirteenth century, often shown as a younger friar holding the infant Jesus, a lily, or a book. He is not the older Egyptian hermit in the Portinari Triptych.

See examples from the Basilica of Saint Anthony

The name Anthony alone is not enough. Check the saint's age, clothing and attributes together—and remember that artists sometimes omit an attribute.

Two Different Saints Named John

John the Baptist

The prophet who preached in the wilderness and baptized Jesus in the Jordan. Common clues include a camel-hair garment, reed cross-staff, a lamb, or a scroll referring to the Lamb of God. The clues connect to his Gospel story.

John the Evangelist

Traditionally associated with the fourth Gospel. Often shown as a youthful apostle, carrying or writing a book, sometimes with an eagle (his Evangelist symbol) or a chalice. He is a separate person from John the Baptist.

Practice Before the Game

Try each clue before opening the answer. These practice items prepare you for the same learning objectives as the game questions.

Which saint is often shown with a dragon?

Saint Margaret of Antioch. The dragon comes from her legend and is a recurring visual attribute.

Which saint is often shown with an ointment jar?

Mary Magdalene. The jar connects with the tradition of anointing Jesus.

Which Anthony has a bell and a T-shaped staff?

Anthony the Abbot, the ancient Egyptian hermit. Anthony of Padua usually has a lily or the Christ Child.

What if all you see is an older woman standing near Mary?

Don't guess based on age. Look for stronger evidence: Anne teaching a young Mary with a book in another artwork, or an identification grounded in the painting's context and provenance.

Look for Prophets Too

Duccio includes Isaiah and Ezekiel in narrow panels beside the Nativity. They lived long before Jesus and are connected with the story through biblical prophecy, rather than being visitors at the birth.

Distinguishing prophets, saints, shepherds and Magi is an important part of recognizing sacred art.
