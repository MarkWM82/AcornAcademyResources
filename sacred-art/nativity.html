The Bible Story — Retold

An original simplified retelling, not an official Bible translation. Read Luke 2:1–7 for the Gospel wording.

At that time, the Roman emperor ordered that people should be registered in a census. Joseph traveled from Nazareth to Bethlehem because his family came from the line of King David. Mary, who was expecting a baby, traveled with him.

While they were in Bethlehem, the time came for Mary's child to be born. She gave birth to her firstborn son, wrapped him in cloths and laid him in a manger—a place where animals could be fed. Luke says that there was no place available for them in the usual lodging.

The account is short and does not tell us every detail of the building or the people who were nearby. Painters often add a stable, a cave, an ox, a donkey or other figures to help us picture the scene. Some of those details come from later Christian art and traditions, rather than directly from Luke.

The important event is the birth of Jesus. In Christian belief, God enters human history in a humble way rather than by arriving as a powerful earthly ruler. Later artists have found many different ways to show that contrast.

Paintings of the Nativity sometimes include other events, such as the angel's announcement to the shepherds. They may also feature prophets or saints who lived at other times. That does not mean the painter thought all those people were physically in Bethlehem.

Different artists may combine events or add symbols, prophets and later saints. We can appreciate those choices while noticing what the Gospel actually says.

Learn Through Art

Explore the paintings and see how artists made choices about people, symbols and the setting. The museum descriptions may include reproductions and further images.

Sandro Botticelli — Mystic Nativity

c. 1500Story and symbols

Angels, olive branches, embraces and fleeing demons make the birth a picture of peace and the defeat of evil. The Magi and shepherds appear together.

A Little Art History

Botticelli painted the Mystic Nativity during a difficult period for Florence, when war and religious ideas shaped how people thought about the future.

The gold dome and dancing angels are not simply a realistic view of Bethlehem. They turn the birth into a vision of heaven celebrating with humanity.

The artist connects Jesus' birth with the hope described in Revelation. This is an interpretation of the wider Christian story, not a detail that Luke includes in his account.

Duccio di Buoninsegna — Nativity with the Prophets Isaiah and Ezekiel

1308–13112 identified saints/prophets

The two figures in the narrow side panels are the prophets Isaiah and Ezekiel. Their scrolls connect Jesus' birth with the Old Testament.

A Little Art History

Duccio made this panel for the great Maestà altarpiece in Siena Cathedral, where it formed part of a sequence of scenes from Jesus' childhood.

The gold background and reclining Mary reflect older Byzantine ways of picturing the Nativity, while the shelter and gestures bring the story close to everyday experience.

The prophets on either side lived long before Jesus. Christians read their writings as pointing forward to the birth; Duccio includes them in the picture to explain that connection.

Fra Angelico and workshop — Nativity (San Marco, Cell 5)

c. 1440–14412 identified saints/prophets

Two saints who lived centuries after Jesus' birth join the scene in prayer. Catherine is associated with a crown and Peter Martyr with a head wound.

A Little Art History

This fresco was painted for Cell 5 of the Dominican convent of San Marco in Florence. Its setting was a place for a friar to pray and reflect.

Mary and Joseph kneel before the child, while angels appear overhead. The quiet arrangement encourages the viewer to contemplate the birth.

Saint Catherine of Alexandria and Saint Peter Martyr appear in this picture, even though Luke does not describe them as witnesses of Jesus' birth. Artists often included later saints in devotional scenes.

Giotto and workshop — Nativity

early 14th centuryStory and symbols

Mary, Jesus, Joseph, angels, a stable and the shepherds' announcement are shown together. The exact source image is still being verified.

A Little Art History

In early Italian painting, artists often arranged several moments from a Bible story inside one picture rather than showing them as separate frames.

The familiar shelter, Mary caring for the infant and the angels announce the essential features of the Nativity.

This reproduction is retained mainly for learning the story. Its exact museum object and attribution will be checked before adding more precise art-history claims.

Fra Angelico — Annunciation and Adoration of the Magi

early 1430sStory and symbols

Two scenes are shown in a single object: Gabriel's visit to Mary and, later, the Magi honoring Jesus.

A Little Art History

This small devotional object brings together two moments from the story of Jesus' coming into the world.

The upper image shows the Annunciation. The lower image presents the visit of the Magi, connecting the promise about Jesus with visitors who recognize him after his birth.

It is one artwork, despite being represented by two photographs in the original source folder. The two stories should be identified separately.
